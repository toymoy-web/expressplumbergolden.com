Why Tree Roots Are a Bigger Sewer Line Problem Near Clear Creek

Golden, CO plumbing guide · Updated 2026-09-07

Why Roots Target Sewer Lines Specifically

Tree roots grow toward moisture and nutrients, and a sewer line carrying wastewater is exactly that: a reliable, nutrient-rich water source running underground. Roots don't need a large opening to get in, a hairline gap at a pipe joint, which is exactly what Golden's bentonite clay soil movement tends to create over time as it shifts seasonally.

Why This Is More Pronounced Near Downtown and Clear Creek

Neighborhoods near Clear Creek and the original downtown Golden grid tend to have both mature, established tree cover, planted decades ago, and older clay or cast-iron sewer lines from the same era, a combination that's essentially made for root intrusion. Newer developments further from downtown generally have younger trees with less-established root systems and often newer PVC sewer lines that resist root intrusion better.

How to Tell If Roots Are Actually the Cause

A camera inspection is the only reliable way to confirm root intrusion versus another cause like a bellied pipe or a simple grease clog. Signs that point toward roots specifically: the clog recurs in the same general area of the yard repeatedly, a snake occasionally comes back with visible root material, or a section of lawn over the sewer line's path is unusually green and lush compared to the rest of the yard.

Snaking vs. Jetting vs. Repair: What Actually Solves It

A mechanical snake punches through roots temporarily but generally leaves much of the root mass intact to regrow within months. Hydro jetting uses high-pressure water to clear roots far more completely from the pipe's interior surface, typically buying meaningfully more time between recurrences. If roots keep returning at the exact same joint despite jetting, that specific joint may need a targeted spot repair rather than repeated clearing.

Should You Remove the Tree Itself?

Removing a mature tree near a problem sewer line is a real option, but it's rarely the first or only step worth taking. A tree that's already sent roots into a line has established root growth that doesn't disappear when the tree above ground is removed, and depending on the species, remaining root systems can persist and keep growing for years afterward.

For a tree that hasn't caused a problem yet but sits directly over a known older sewer line, a root barrier installed between the tree and the pipe is a less drastic middle option. It physically redirects new root growth away from the line without requiring you to remove a mature, established tree over a purely preventive concern.

The decision usually comes down to how much the tree matters to you and how many times the same line has already needed clearing. A tree that's caused three root-intrusion clogs in two years is a different conversation than one that's never caused an issue but happens to sit near an aging line.

What This Costs Over Time If You Do Nothing

Repeatedly snaking the same root-intrusion clog every few months adds up faster than most homeowners realize, especially once you factor in the water damage risk each time the line backs up before you notice. Two or three snaking calls a year, over several years, often exceeds the cost of a single hydro jetting service or a targeted spot repair that actually addresses the underlying cause.

There's also a compounding risk worth considering: each cycle of root growth and clearing puts additional stress on an already-aging joint, and a joint that's been repeatedly disturbed is more likely to eventually need a full repair rather than continued clearing. Addressing it properly once is usually the more economical path, even though the upfront cost of jetting or repair is higher than another round of snaking.

Root Intrusion Diagnosis Checklist

  • Note whether clogs recur in the same location repeatedly
  • Check for unusually green or lush lawn patches over the sewer line's path
  • Ask for a camera inspection rather than repeated blind snaking
  • Ask whether hydro jetting has been tried before assuming a full repair is needed
  • If jetting doesn't resolve it long-term, ask about a targeted spot repair at the specific joint

Frequently Asked Questions

Will removing the tree solve the problem?

It can help prevent future intrusion at that specific spot, but existing roots in the line won't disappear on their own and still need to be cleared or the section repaired.

Is hydro jetting safe for the older clay pipe common near downtown Golden?

Generally yes when pressure is calibrated correctly, which is why we check pipe condition with a camera first on older lines before jetting.

Are root barriers effective if roots have already reached the pipe?

A root barrier mainly prevents new intrusion going forward; for a line that already has root growth inside it, that existing growth still needs to be cleared through jetting or repair regardless of any barrier installed afterward.

How often should a sewer line be camera-inspected preventively?

For an older home with mature trees nearby, every 2-3 years is a reasonable interval; homes with no history of root issues and newer PVC lines can generally go longer between checks.

Does hydro jetting need to be repeated regularly as maintenance?

Not typically as routine maintenance for a healthy line, though a property with a documented history of root intrusion sometimes benefits from a scheduled jetting every few years as preventive care rather than waiting for the next clog.
